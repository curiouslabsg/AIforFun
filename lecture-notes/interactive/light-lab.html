01

Reflection

Measure every angle from the normal, the dashed line at 90° to the mirror. The law of reflection says the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection.

i = r

Now tilt the mirror. The normal tilts with it, so the angle of incidence changes, and so does the angle of reflection on the other side. The reflected ray swings through twice the tilt.

Exam trap: “30° to the mirror” means i = 90° − 30° = 60°.

02

Refraction

Light slows down in glass and water. When it crosses the boundary at an angle, the change of speed bends it. Slower means it bends towards the normal; faster means away.

n = sin i / sin r = c / v

The bigger the refractive index, the more the light slows and the more it bends. Diamond slows light to about half its speed in air.

Quick check: the bigger angle is always the one in air. If you get n < 1, you divided the wrong way round.
Try this: set the angle to 0°. The ray goes straight through without bending, but the speed readout still drops.

03

Total internal reflection

Now send the light the other way: from inside the glass out into air. It bends away from the normal. Increase the angle and the escaping ray gets flatter, until at the critical angle it skims along the surface.

sin c = 1 / n

Go past the critical angle and nothing escapes. All the light reflects back inside. That needs two things together: light heading from a denser medium into a less dense one, and i > c.

Try this: switch to diamond. Its critical angle is only 24.4°, so light inside a cut diamond gets trapped and bounces around, which is why it sparkles.

Optical fibres

A glass core sits inside cladding with a lower refractive index. Light that hits the core wall at more than the critical angle is totally internally reflected, again and again, all the way along. Launch it too steeply and it leaks out.

That is how almost all internet traffic between countries travels, and how an endoscope lets a doctor see inside your stomach without surgery.

04

Thin converging lens

A converging lens bends rays parallel to the axis so they meet at the principal focus F. The focal length f is the distance from the lens centre to F.

To find an image, draw two rays from the tip of the object:

1. parallel to the axis, then through F
2. straight through the centre of the lens

Where they cross is the image. If they spread apart instead, trace them back with dashed lines: the image is virtual.
